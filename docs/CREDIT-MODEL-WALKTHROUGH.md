# The credit model, step by step

_Generated by `python -m case_analytics.credit.docs --write` from the snapshot, so every number is this book's. Drafted by Claude Code; review it like code. Each step ends with a question to check yourself._

## 1. The target

Default means the Solar loan reached 30 or more days past due within 18 months: the `dpd30` flag. On the funded test book that happened to 225 of 10,000 loans (2.25%). It is not the Mortgage `label__defaulted`, which belongs to a different generator and a different product.

*Check yourself:* why is a default flag, rather than the simulation's PD, the thing a lender can train on?

## 2. The split

Training uses 8 independent books (80,000 loans, 2,004 defaults); calibration uses two more; the locked seed-42 book is the test set and is touched once, at the end. Splitting by book rather than by row means no applicant, dealer draw or seed is shared between training and test. Tuning folds also hold out whole books.

*Check yourself:* what would go wrong if the test book were used to choose the hyperparameters?

## 3. The features

Fourteen decision-time fields: the stated figures, the verified ones from the documents, and one-sided discrepancies between them (for example `income_overstatement_pct`, the positive part of stated minus verified income, over verified). An underwriter has both figures, so the discrepancies are allowed. Excluded: sensitive and geographic attributes, dealer ID, device metadata, fraud-rule outputs, W-2 wages and every hidden-truth column.

*Check yourself:* why is dealer ID excluded even though dealer quality drives default in this simulation?

## 4. The baseline

Logistic regression on standardised features: each coefficient is the change in log-odds for one standard deviation of the feature. The largest here: `income_overstatement_pct` +0.302; `large_deposits_total` +0.166; `undisclosed_debt` +0.049. Test AUC 0.631 (0.591–0.673).

*Check yourself:* a coefficient of −0.5 on `fico_bureau`: what happens to the odds of default for a one-SD higher score?

## 5. XGBoost

A boosted model adds small trees one at a time, each correcting the last. Monotonic constraints force a higher FICO never to raise risk and a larger discrepancy never to lower it, so the model cannot learn a pattern a credit committee would reject. Classes are not reweighted: reweighting would inflate every prediction and they would stop being probabilities. Chosen settings: depth 2, 150 trees at learning rate 0.05.

*Check yourself:* why does `scale_pos_weight` = 1 matter for a PD model but not for a fraud-ranking model?

## 6. Evaluation

AUC is the chance a random defaulter scores above a random non-defaulter: `credit_pd_xgb` 0.640 (0.600–0.678). KS is the largest gap between the cumulative score distributions of defaulters and non-defaulters: 0.272 (0.218–0.337). Brier is the mean squared error of the probabilities: 0.0215 (0.0188–0.0243). Calibration by decile compares mean predicted PD with the observed rate in each tenth of the book; in the riskiest decile here 6.52% was predicted and 7.90% observed. **`credit_pd_xgb` does not clearly beat the logistic baseline.** Its AUC is higher by 0.008, but the paired 95% interval (-0.024 to 0.043) contains 0, so this test book of 225 defaults cannot tell them apart.

*Check yourself:* a model with high AUC but poor calibration: which committee question can it answer, and which can't it?

## 7. SHAP

Each prediction's log-odds equal a base value (-3.666, which is a PD of 1 / (1 + e^3.666) ≈ 2.5%) plus one contribution per feature; this was checked on all 10,000 test loans. Contributions are summed by group so one cause is not reported twice, and the groups that raise risk most become reasons. Largest groups on average: INCOME_DISCREPANCY 0.1915; UNSOURCED_DEPOSITS 0.0614; CREDIT_SCORE 0.0285. Across 20 retrains on resampled training data, a test loan's top reason matched the headline model's 54% of the time on average; 14% of loans kept the same top reason in at least 80% of retrains and 39% in fewer than half. Over all loans the top reason is **unstable**: most loans sit near the average PD, where every contribution is small and the ranking flips easily. In the riskiest tenth of the book, where a reason statement would actually be sent, agreement averages 89% (84% of those loans at 80% or more): stable.

*Check yourself:* why sum `dti_stated` and `stated_monthly_debts` into one reason?

## 8. Learned PD against true PD

The simulation knows each loan's true probability. `credit_pd_xgb`'s predictions rank-correlate 0.196 with it, and 0.569 with the true PD averaged over the dealers each loan could have been assigned to. The fair ceiling, that dealer-averaged true PD itself, reaches AUC 0.650 (0.619–0.683): that is how close a dealer-blind model could get.

*Check yourself:* why can't a real lender run this comparison?

## 9. Reason accuracy

For loans whose falsified field is known, did the top reason name it? On the funded test book's 357 income-inflation loans the top reason was the income discrepancy 357 times (100%). That score is perfect by construction: in this simulation only fraud loans have an income gap, while real files have benign ones (bonuses, rounding, pay changes). Reasons are stated only above a materiality threshold (0.005412 log-odds, chosen on the calibration books), which cut false discrepancy reasons on clean test loans from 41.8% to 4.1%. The model card has every typology, the teaching twins and the clean controls.

*Check yourself:* if reason accuracy were low but AUC high, what would you keep beside the model?

## 10. Limits

This model recovers a simple, known simulated process; its numbers do not transfer to real data. It learns from funded loans only, has no out-of-time test, has fairness evidence that is monitoring only (step 11), and is a second opinion beside the credit policy, never the decision. It must not be used for real lending.

The policy view shows the funded-only limit in money. **Why the logistic model's choice costs $7,064,313 against the policy's $4,413,431.** Trained on funded loans only, under a policy that already screens FICO and DTI, the model learns that credit score barely matters: its FICO coefficient is -0.038 per standard deviation. Applied to every applicant, the linear model extrapolates that flat slope to people the policy declined: of its 3,415 swap-ins, 1,466 were policy declines and 5.5% have a FICO below 660; their mean charge-off PD is 3.40% against 1.54% for the loans it swaps out. Monotonic trees flatten beyond the data instead of extrapolating a line, so `credit_pd_xgb` swaps in 1,134 (0.4% below FICO 660) at about the same PD as it swaps out, and costs $4,445,121. Both are scoring applicants outside their training population; the reject-inference section measures how much of that standard methods recover. The comparison also favours the policy: it reads the claimed FICO, which in this generator equals the true score, while both models read the noisier bureau score. So the models' loss figures are conservative, and part of the logistic model's $7,064,313 is that handicap, not only selection bias.

*Check yourself:* name two things a real model risk review would ask for that this sprint does not provide.

## 11. Fairness

Three checks. **Counterfactual flips:** change one sensitive attribute and nothing else, and every output must stay the same; a test-only policy that reads age fails, so the check can fail. Here every flip of every case left every output unchanged, apart from the region flip's housing cost, which moves DTI (the financial channel, reported on its own). **Group outcomes:** approval rate by group and the adverse impact ratio, the group's rate over the highest group's. Under the champion the lowest age band is 22-29, at 0.660. **Proxy check:** can the 14 features predict the attribute? For region the AUC is 0.937, so a model could reproduce regional differences without seeing region.

*Check yourself:* the flips show no engine reads age, yet applicants aged 22-29 are approved at 0.660 of the highest group's rate. Is that proof of fair treatment?

## 12. Champion/challenger

A fair comparison holds everything equal except the decision rule: the same applicants, the same approval count and the same information. The app's policy reads the claimed FICO, which here is the true score, so the champion is the bureau-score policy instead: the same rules on the score the models see. Challenger A cannot be told apart from the champion on expected loss: the difference is +$17,067, and its 95% interval (−$137,110 to +$190,082) contains 0. Challenger B beats the champion: expected loss is lower by $743,193 (95% interval $635,143 to $857,582). The overlay changes only 500 of 9,991 approvals.

*Check yourself:* why can a policy-plus-model overlay beat the model on its own, when the model ranks risk better than the policy's thresholds?

## 13. Reject inference

A lender sees outcomes only for loans it made. Reject inference guesses outcomes for the rest and retrains. Here every application has an outcome, so the guesses can be checked: for `credit_pd_xgb` the expected loss of the chosen book is $4,432,345 funded-only, $5,971,649 with hard cut-off augmentation, $4,500,634 with fuzzy augmentation and $3,821,795 with the oracle's true outcomes.

*Check yourself:* why can't augmentation that labels declined applicants with the funded-only model's own PD teach the new model anything the old one did not know?

## 14. Drift

PSI (population stability index) compares a feature's distribution today with the training data, bin by bin: under 0.10 is stable, 0.10 to 0.25 is worth watching, above 0.25 calls for action. It needs no outcomes, so it can warn on the day loans are funded. Performance counts as decayed if the calibration test fails (observed defaults against the model's expected count) or the AUC test fires (the 95% interval of the book's AUC minus the test book's, from independent bootstraps of both, lies wholly below 0). **Control (unchanged):** PSI is under 0.10 on every feature and on the score, as an unchanged book must be. Performance also holds: calibration held (249 defaults observed against 251 expected) and the AUC test did not fire (AUC 0.584 against the test book's 0.640, a difference of −0.056 with a 95% CI of −0.115 to +0.002, an interval that reaches 0 or above). **Lower FICO:** the monitors warned alongside the decay. PSI reached the watch line on `fico_bureau` (score PSI 0.090). Performance decayed: calibration is off (281 defaults observed against 250 expected) and ranking weakened (AUC 0.577 against the test book's 0.640, a difference of −0.062 with a 95% CI of −0.118 to −0.006, wholly below 0). **More fraud:** no warning and no measurable decay: calibration held (306 defaults observed against 292 expected) and the AUC test did not fire (AUC 0.650 against the test book's 0.640, a difference of +0.010 with a 95% CI of −0.043 to +0.065, an interval that reaches 0 or above). **Weaker dealers:** **the shift hurt performance without moving PSI.** No feature and not the score reached 0.10 (largest feature PSI 0.002), yet calibration is off (573 defaults observed against 250 expected).

*Check yourself:* in the weaker-dealer book no feature's PSI reaches 0.10, yet the model under-predicts defaults. Why can't PSI see it, and what would?

## Answer key

1. A lender observes whether a loan defaulted, never its probability; the PD exists only inside the simulation.
2. The hyperparameters would fit the test book's noise, so its score would be optimistic and stop being an honest estimate of performance on new loans.
3. The model would memorise dealers instead of learning borrower risk, and a new dealer would have no history. Dealer risk is watched separately, in the Dealer View.
4. The odds multiply by e^−0.5 ≈ 0.61: about 39% lower odds of default for a score one standard deviation higher.
5. Reweighting changes the default rate the model sees, so its outputs stop being probabilities; pricing, expected loss and capital need real probabilities, while a ranking model only needs the order.
6. It can answer "does it rank risk?" (who is riskier) but not "how many will default?" (what to price or provision for), which needs calibration.
7. Both measure one cause, the debt burden; stated separately they would repeat one reason and crowd out a different one.
8. Only the simulation knows each loan's true probability; a lender sees outcomes only.
9. The fraud rules and document checks, which name the falsified field directly.
10. Any two of: independent validation; out-of-time and drift testing; fairness and disparate-impact testing; reject inference; real data.
11. No. The flips prove only that no engine reads age. The difference arrives through the decision inputs (FICO, DTI, liquidity and fraud review) as they vary with age, so it is evidence for a reviewer to weigh (is each factor justified by risk, and is there a less discriminatory alternative?), not proof of fair or unfair treatment.
12. Because the champion sees information the model does not: its review routing (fraud referrals, the DTI watch band and the liquidity check) comes from the fraud rules and the documents, which are not model inputs. Challenger A throws that away and approves applications the policy sent to fraud review; the overlay keeps it and lets the model remove the riskiest approvals, which here are mostly undetected fraud.
13. Augmentation labels the declined applicants with the funded-only model's own scores, so it can only teach the new model what the old one already believed, including its bias. Only real outcomes for declined applicants (the oracle here; in practice a test-and-learn sample of approvals below the cut-off, or bureau outcomes on loans taken elsewhere) add information.
14. The dealer is not a model input and no applicant changes, so every feature's distribution, and the score's, stays where it was: PSI has nothing to see. Outcome monitors do: calibration-in-the-large on early delinquency, and delinquency by dealer (the Dealer View's ratings), which watch the channel rather than the applicant.
